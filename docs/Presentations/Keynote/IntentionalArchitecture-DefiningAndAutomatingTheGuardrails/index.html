Challenge Conventional Wisdom

Robert Goddard challenged the idea that rockets needed air to push against in order to create motion. A January 1920 New York Times editorial implied he clearly knew this and must be intentionally misleading us.

Goddard wasn't challenging Newtonian physics; he was questioning the misconception that the consequences of Newton's law were that rockets could not operate in the vacuum of space. He tested his ideas through experiments and demonstrated that this was not actually such a consequence.

NY Times Editorial

"That Professor Goddard...does not know the relation of action to reaction...would be absurd. Of course, he only seems to lack the knowledge ladled out daily in high schools" - The New York Times, January 1920

  • Everyone Knows
    • Rockets need air to push against.
    • The vacuum of space makes flight impossible.
  • Goddard's challenge
    • Rockets that could operate beyond the atmosphere.
    • In 1920, a New York Times editorial mocked his efforts.

Free Thinking, Scientific Guardrails

Goddard's breakthroughs followed an engineering loop. Propose a theory outside of conventional wisdom, compute what that predicts from Newton's 3rd law and conservation of momentum, compare that with experiments, then iteratively build on those results, ultimately resulting in the Apollo space program and a rare apology from the NYT.

  • Theory: Rockets can work in a vacuum
    • Unconstrained: challenge the assumption
  • Predict the outcome
    • Newton's third law and conservation of momentum
    • Exhaust goes back \(\rightarrow\) rocket goes forward
  • Test the prediction
    • Vacuum-chamber experiments and measurements
  • Evidence decides validity
    • Guardrail: Results of experiment
    • Neither conviction nor ridicule count

Meanwhile, Professor Feynman

In Messenger Lecture #7, Feynman describes making a new law as a cycle: guess a possible law, calculate what it predicts, and compare that prediction with experiment. When the prediction disagrees with observation, the proposed law must be discarded or revised. The calculation makes an idea testable; the experiment gives reality the final say. Creativity starts the cycle, but does not exempt an idea from the results of experiments.

Feynman proposed a machine for this process but almost immediately rejected it as impractical, because while machines can permute possibilities, they cannot originate new ideas or perform the creative step required to start the cycle.

At least they couldn't until now...

  • Process for Theoretical Physics
    • Guess a law that might explain nature.
    • Compute its consequences.
    • Compare predictions with observation.
    • Discard it if prediction and observation disagree.
  • Feynman Proposed (and rejected) a machine for this
    • Guessing the laws of nature requires creative insight
    • Mechanical variation is not enough
    • A machine can permute possibilities
      • It cannot originate a testable hypothesis.
      • At least, it couldn't back then...

Newton's First Law

Let's look at the example proposed by Professor Feynman in the same lecture series. He explained that Newton's First Law, about objects in motion staying in motion, is not of known origin. Let's consider some possible explanations:

  • Just the way it is - universal default
  • Angels push with their wings
  • Movement warps space similar to gravity
  • Momentum is a substance transferred into an object

An object in motion stays in motion until acted on by a force - Sir Isaac Newton

The reason why things coast forever has never been found out -- the law of inertia has no known origin. - Richard Feynman

Predictions $\rightarrow$ Guardrails

These examples do not constrain the first guess. Goddard's experiment came at the end of the loop: compare predictions with immutable physics, then keep, revise, or discard the idea. Feynman's cycle likewise lets a guess start freely, then asks whether a testable prediction survives comparison with observation through the scientific method. AI changes the scale and speed of generation, not the presence of understanding: it brings no context, intent, or comprehension of its own. Software has no natural boundary beyond the runtime. We must intentionally define and automate the boundaries our tools and organizations need.

  • Scientists & Engineers:
    • Theories need testable predictions
    • Experiments compare ideas with reality
  • Generative AI:
    • Generates ideas at scale and speed
    • Limited context and intent
    • No understanding
  • Software:
    • Is constrained only by the runtime
    • We must define testable predictions
    • We must provide the means of judging them

Baseline Guardrails

It is surprising how much a good compiler, along with a solid type system can catch. That said, it is not nearly enough. These baseline guardrails are essential but we need to protect ourselves, our companies, and our users much more broadly than we can with just basic construct validation and business-rules checks.

Tool Validation
Compilers & linters Reject invalid syntax
Unit tests Validate behavior
Integration/acceptance tests Higher-level behavior
Data contracts Edge behavior

We Need Even Better Boundaries

As we move to the latest, most capable, Frontier models, we may need fewer skills or workflow steps that teach them how to do a task. Context engineering can give a model the information it needs to make a better choice, but giving a model an instruction is not the same as enforcing a rule. Software must still enforce project, organizational, and compliance requirements. Project guardrails evolve with the project. Architectural guardrails should be more stable and should not need to change just because the model does.

  • Better models may need fewer skills.
  • Context informs decisions.
  • Guardrails enforce policy and safety.
  • What kinds of guardrails can we create?

Other Models Review, Not Enforce

Combining models with other tools can expose issues that a single model misses. A different model can review code or assess an output against stated requirements, offering another perspective alongside human review and automated checks. That review is still stochastic. Models can miss the same issue, produce false positives, or agree on an incorrect answer. Use model review as a stopgap while building enforceable checks, not as proof that a rule holds. The next example shows why guidance and review alone cannot establish the boundaries a system needs. All the code reviews in the world would not have stopped the Jul 2026 Hugging Face incident.

  • Use another model to review code and validate outputs
  • Combine with human review and automated tools
  • A second model may find issues the first missed
  • Its judgment is still stochastic and can be wrong
  • Fixing those problems may result in others
  • Treat review as a stopgap, not an enforced guardrail

AIs "Go Rogue"?

In July 2026, agents in an OpenAI cyber evaluation reached Hugging Face's production infrastructure from an environment intended to isolate them. The failure was that the agents could act outside the authorized test environment, not that they lacked the ability to do the task. Network boundaries and enforced proxies limit what agents can reach without relying on the model to follow instructions. Logs support investigation and audits, while anomaly detection helps operators spot unexpected activity. Better models may need less guidance, but operators still need independent controls and oversight. Source: AI Tools Review's incident summary.

  • July 2026: OpenAI agents penetrate Hugging Face.
  • Network boundaries were unconstrained.
  • Detect anomalies and audit activity with logs.
  • AIs don't "go rogue".
  • They follow the route we map.
  • We forgot to mark that cliff...

Operational Guardrails

Baseline checks validate code and tested behavior, but they do not themselves limit everything a running system can access or do. The preceding example shows why boundaries must also be enforced at runtime. In an enterprise, these are typically the purview of the operations, platform engineering, security, and compliance teams, who enforce runtime policies and monitor system behavior to ensure adherence to organizational standards.

Guardrail Purpose
Policy enforcement Govern allowed behavior
Network controls Restrict network access
Authorization Restrict resource access
CI/CD gates Prevent non-compliant releases
Runtime monitoring Detect drift in production

Architectural Guardrails

Architectural guardrails make design choices explicit and checkable. Dependency and layering rules constrain relationships between components. Fitness functions check architectural intent over time, while static analysis can detect violations and known anti-patterns before deployment. Framework constraints make desired designs easier to follow. These checks complement operational controls over release and runtime behavior.

Guardrail Purpose
Dependency rules Who can talk to whom
Layering rules Enforce boundaries
Fitness functions Continuously verify intent
Static analysis Other compile-time checks
Anti-pattern detection Identify known bad designs
Framework constraints Make the right thing easy

Be Your Own Guardrails First

I know there are many people in this room who are already experts at AI assisted development. For those who are NOT, I strongly recommend being the guardrails yourself first before trying to automate them.

My personal rule is: never automate something you haven't done yourself at least twice.

I was very fortunate that, at the time these tools were first being released, I was working at a company that said that nobody knew how these tools would end up being used, and encouraged us to explore and experiment with them so we had an understanding of what they could and couldn't do well.

People who jump straight to:

press a button -- get an application

are bypassing the mechanics of software development with AI.

It's like becoming a building inspector before you've ever used a hammer or saw.

Consistency

  • Data Consistency: An order status, payment state, and shipment state are not contradictory.

  • System Consistency: Every observer of the system sees the same data and relationships.

Data is consistent when its value and related representations obey the rules for that data.

Systems are consistent when all of its data and relationships obey the system's rules for that data.

Eventual Consistency

Eliminates temporal coupling

  • Enables Patterns & Practices that can improve

    • Resiliency & Fault Tolerance
      • Failures are isolated and self-healing
    • Maintainability & Extensibility
      • Easier to change individual components without disrupting others
    • Performance & Scalability
      • Components operate and scale independently

Store & Forward

Embracing Eventual Consistency enables the use of patterns like Store & Forward

Get it safe, then get it done

  • Do no work until the data is persisted
  • Verify the message is well-formed
  • Do not verify the message is valid if that requires work
  • Once stored, return "Accepted"
    • i.e. HTTP 202

Consistency

Embrace Eventual Consistency

  • There is no such thing as "Full Consistency"
  • Our systems RARELY need more than eventual consistency
  • Don't force your users or other systems to wait for an answer
  • Get it safe, then get it done

Consistency Conversation

Development teams should have conversations around Consistency that are primarily focused around making certain that the system is assumed to be eventually consistency throughout.

Consistency Questions
  • What patterns and tools will we use to create systems that support reliable, eventually consistent operations?
    • Example: Reliable messaging using ASB or Cosmos Change-Feed
  • How will we identify areas where stronger consistency has been wedged-in and should be removed?
    • Example: Look for polling, distributed trx, etc
  • How will we prevent future demands for strong consistency, either explicit or assumed, from weakening our systems?
    • Example: Document our commitment to Eventual Consistency in an ADR and explicitly discuss in code reviews
  • How will we identify when there are unusual or unacceptable delays in reaching consistency?
    • Example: Data Freshness SLIs/SLOs
  • How will we communicate the status of the system and any delays in reaching consistency to the stakeholders?
    • Example: Dashboards or toast notifications

Context

The Context is the setting in which a software-intensive system exists and operates. It includes the system's interactions with users, other systems, and its environment. It can be difficult to define and is often overlooked in system design. Fortunately, there are processes to help us.

The boundary within which a system or sub-system operates

  • Actors
    • User
    • Other Sub-systems
    • Other Systems
  • Entities
    • Owned (Read/Write)
    • Reference (Read only)

Bounded Context

If we do a good job of defining our Bounded Contexts, we'll have fewer conflicts and less confusion. However, this is not the only kind of Context we need to care about. We also need to consider the Execution Context.

A single business grammar

  • Every term has exactly one meaning
  • Example: Bus
    • Work Order Context: A vehicle needing diagostics or repair
      • Service Time
      • Repair History
    • Inventory Context: A depreciable asset
      • In-Service date
      • Manufacturer and Type
  • Terms are captured in Ubiquitous Language

Execution Context

  • The unit of work of all services

  • The life-cycle of a single request

  • The key to service reliability

Two At-A-Time

An ACID compliant transaction is a single change, just don't abuse that to the detriment of your performance, availability & scalability

Is is NOT possible to reliably make more than one change to system state in a single execution context

Two changes can be made to system state in a single execution context if:

  • The 1st change is idempotent
  • The 2nd change is unreliable

Idempotence

We should make our services idempotent when reasonable to do so

  • Improves Reliability
    • Retry at will
  • May Hurt Maintainability & Extensibility
    • Once published or utilized, it becomes part of the contract
  • Can be abused
    • Unintentional DoS attacks

Context

We must define our Bounded Contexts well and defend our Execution contexts

  • Utilize Event Storming
  • Avoid Dual-Writes
  • Leverage Idempotence when possible

Context Conversation

Development teams should have conversations around Context that are primarily focused around the tools and techniques that they intend to use to define their Bounded Contexts and to avoid the Dual-Writes Anti-Pattern.

Context Questions

  • What database technologies will we use and how can we leverage these tools to create downstream events based on changes to the database state?

  • Which of our services are currently idempotent and which ones could reasonably made so? How can we leverage our idempotent services to improve system reliability?

  • Do we have any services right now that contain business processes implemented in a less-reliable way? If so, pulling this functionality out into their own microservices might be a good starting point for decomposition.

  • What processes will we as a development team implement to track and manage the technical debt of having business processes implemented in a less-reliable way?

  • What processes will we implement to be sure that any future less-reliable implementations of business functionality are made only after strong consideration and with a plan to pay it off, appropriate documentation and prioritization by the business and product owner.

Contract

It's easy to fall into the trap of assuming that OpenAPI or AsyncAPI specs are all we need for contracts. Those specs generally only deal with the 1st 2 items, and often the last 2 are completely ignored.

An agreement between a service provider and the service's consumers

  • Operations

  • Data Types & Structures

  • Quality of Service

    • i.e. SLAs

  • Versioning Strategy

    • i.e. compatibility

  • Behavior

    • i.e. idempotence

The Canonical Model

Empowering innovation in the shadows, while ensuring stability in the spotlight

Maintain a distinct internal model

  • Preserve team agility
  • Stability for Consumers
  • Robustness to Change
  • Only one service should write
  • Only services in THIS SUBSYSTEM should read

Contract

Once a message is defined, all stakeholders have a compatibility expectation

  • Protect your internal, isolated stream for rapid iteration
  • Use upstream contracts to bring external data to local stores
  • Avoid creating predicate queries for downstream use

Contract Conversation

Development teams should have conversations around Contract that are primarily focused around creating processes that define any integration contracts for both upstream and downstream services, and serve to defend their internal data representations and implementations against any external consumers.

Contract Questions
  • How will we isolate our internal data representations from those of our downstream consumers?
  • What types of compatibility guarantees are our tools and practices capable of providing?
  • What procedures should we have in place to monitor incoming and outgoing contracts for compatibility?
  • What should our procedures look like for making a change to a stream that has downstream consumers?
  • How can we leverage upstream messaging contracts to further reduce the coupling of our systems to our upstream dependencies?

Chaos

Software will fail -- in the worst possible way and at the worst possible time

  • Have low expectations of commodity infrastructure

    • Networks will segment
    • Pods, Servers and Drives will fail
    • Containers and OSs will become unstable

  • Remember The Fallacies of Distributed Computing

Embrace the Chaos

Thought experiments are useful, but you cannot really know how a system will respond to different types of failures until you have those failures in production. Historically, such "tests" have occurred at random, at the whim of the infrastructure, and usually at the worst possible time. Instead of leaving these things to chance, tools like Chaos Monkey can be used to simulate failures in production, and can be configured to create these failures during times where the appropriate support engineers are available and ready to respond if necessary.

Embrace the fact that failures will occur

  • Analytical Testing

    • FMEA
    • Virtual Chaos Engineering

  • Empirical Testing

    • Leverage Feature Flags
    • Simian Army (Chaos Monkey, etc)

  • Start by Testing in Lower Environments

Build Self-Healing Systems

Chaos engineering exposes failures, but a well-architected system also needs a way to recover from them without an operator intervening every time. Synchronous retries can handle transient failures, provided they are bounded and use backoff so they do not amplify an outage. When work can be asynchronous, durable messaging can decouple a consumer from its dependencies in time. If processing fails, roll back the processing transaction or leave the message unacknowledged so it can be redelivered, according to the messaging system's guarantees. Idempotent handling protects against duplicate delivery. Monitor consumer lag and alert when recovery stalls, since a growing backlog is not self-healing. This approach is often preferable for work that can tolerate delay, but it does not replace synchronous recovery where an immediate answer is required.

  • Recover from failures with minimal intervention
  • Synchronous retries can handle transient failures
    • Bound retries and use backoff
  • Prefer asynch recovery when work can tolerate delay
    • Roll back failed processing and redeliver messages
    • Make processing idempotent
  • Monitor consumer lag and alert when recovery stalls

Chaos Conversation

Development teams should have conversations around Chaos that are primarily focused around procedures for identifying and remediating possible failure points in the application.

Chaos Questions

  • How can we make our systems self-healing?

  • How will we evaluate potential sources of failures in our systems before they are built?

    • How will we handle the inability to reach a dependency such as a database?
    • How will we handle duplicate messages sent from our upstream data sources?
    • How will we handle messages sent out-of-order from our upstream data sources?

  • How will we expose possible sources of failures during any pre-deployment testing?

  • How will we expose possible sources of failures in the production environment before they occur for users?

  • How will we identify errors that occur for users within production?

  • How will we prioritize changes to the system based on the results of these experiments?

Competencies & Coalescence

The last 2 categories are more about what we can learn, and how we can leverage these models before and after the build process, and thus may fit better with the operational type guardrails. They include identifying key areas to build and gathering the key operational data needed for effective decision-making and system improvement.

  • Competencies: The skills and capabilities that differentiate us from our competitors
    • Focus on processes that:
      • Are unique to our business
      • Change frequently
      • Are complex parts of the domain
  • Coalescence: Automate gathering operational data
    • Deployment & Verification Testing
    • Logging, Tracing, & Monitoring
    • Feature Flagging
    • Dependency Status

Competencies Conversation

Development teams should have conversations around Competencies that are primarily focused around what systems, sub-systems, and components should be built, which should be installed off-the-shelf, and what libraries or infrastructure capabilities should be utilized.

Competencies Questions
  • What are our core competencies?
  • How do we identify "build vs. buy" opportunities?
  • How do we make "build vs. buy" decisions on needed systems?
  • How do we identify cross-cutting concerns and infrastructure capabilites that can be leveraged?
  • How do we determine which libraries or infrastructure components will be utilized?
  • How do we manage the versioning of utilized components, especially in regard to security updates?
  • How do we document our decisions for later review?

Coalescence Conversation

Development teams should have conversations around Coalescence that are primarily focused around what data streams are available that provide insight into the system, and how to make them available easily to all who might need them.

Questions About Coalescence
  • What is our mechanism for deployment and system verification?
  • What is our mechanism for logging/traceability within our system?
  • How will we increase the level of logging when needed?
  • How will we expose SLIs and other metrics so they are available when needed?
  • How will we identify the status of dependencies so we can understand when our systems are reacting to downstream anomalies?
  • Are there ways to perform ad-hoc queries against logs, metrics or dependencies to provide additional insight in an outage?
  • How will we know when there are anomalies in our logs, metrics or dependencies?
  • How will we coalesce our logs, metrics and dependency statuses for easy access?

Principles → Enforced Guardrails

Unit tests are useful for protecting application behavior, especially business rules. They do not consistently protect architectural invariants. The Critical Cs framework makes architectural expectations explicit. Existing tools can turn those principles into repeatable checks. AST-based rules can enforce dependency, layering, and naming boundaries, while linters, compilers, ArchUnit, Semgrep, and similar tools provide other forms of early enforcement. These checks are useful for both human and AI-assisted development. The machine-readable framework helps people and tools identify candidate guardrails, but teams must translate principles into configured checks that run consistently.

  • Unit tests validate behavior and business rules.
  • Validations also needed for architectural principles.
    • Critical Cs expose desired principles.

Tools for Building Guardrails

It's easy to think about Unit Tests as guardrails, but they are specific to the application. The principles we are discussing here are more far-reaching. Existing tools can help turn invariant properties into repeatable checks, including for AI-generated changes, though not all tools are available in all languages and for all frameworks. The machine-readable Critical Cs framework describes dimensions, concepts, and principles that people and tools can use to guide an assessment and identify candidate guardrails. Note: The Critical Cs YAML is not itself an enforcement mechanism: teams must translate relevant principles into checks and configure those checks to run.

  • Semgrep: check code against custom patterns
  • ArchUnit: test dependency and layer rules
  • Others depending on language/ecosystem
  • Abstract Syntax Trees (AST): custom static analysis rules

Abstract Syntax Trees

An abstract syntax tree (AST) represents source code as a hierarchy of meaningful constructs rather than as a sequence of characters. Parsers produce trees like this, although the exact node names and details vary by language and tool. In this simplified example, the assignment has a target, total, and a value. That value is a multiplication whose left and right operands are price and quantity. The tree preserves these relationships while leaving out formatting details such as whitespace. As introduced on the previous slide, tools can inspect code structures like these to find patterns and enforce guardrails. The next step is to turn an architectural rule into a check that can run on changes.

  • A tree of meaningful code structures
  • Nodes represent constructs:
    • Assignments
    • Expressions
    • Declarations
    • Control structures
    • Literals
    • etc.
  • Edges show how those constructs relate
  • Tools inspect the tree to check code against rules

Free Thinking, Testable Guardrails

The guardrail is not conventional thinking; it is the requirement that an idea expose itself to a test. Feynman's cycle protects us from our own cleverness because an elegant explanation still has to survive comparison with observation. This parallels Goddard: his unconventional rocket idea was free to challenge assumptions, while immutable physics and experiments determined whether it worked. Goddard tested a mechanism; Feynman's process tests proposed laws. In both cases, evidence makes bold thinking trustworthy.

  • Imagine freely
    • Propose explanations beyond convention.
  • Make each idea testable
    • Derive predicted consequences
  • Let evidence decide
    • Elegance doesn't change anything
    • Who proposed it is immaterial
    • Cleverness or derision doesn't matter
  • Similar Processes - Similar Guardrails
    • Goddard tested rocket propulsion in a vacuum.
    • Feynman tested laws against observation.

Understand Before You Automate

Reviewing AI-generated work yourself shows which mistakes recur in your project, which checks catch them, and which outputs rarely need edits. An output can still carry risk even if it rarely needs correction. Experienced AI Engineers can help identify common mistakes that need guardrails. We'll explore a few examples today, but nothing can substitute for strong intuition in this area.

  • What mistakes recur?
  • What do I always review?
  • Which architectural rules do I enforce?
  • Which tests catch the most problems?
  • Which outputs rarely need edits?
  • Which risks still need safeguards?
