Challenge Conventional Wisdom

A public misconception held that rockets needed air to push against, so they could not work in a vacuum. Robert Goddard challenged that assumption with his work on rocketry. A January 1920 New York Times editorial mocked his "mis"-understanding of basic physics, implying that he was intentionally misleading us for some nefarious purpose. Q: What separates an unconventional idea from an unsupported guess? NY Times Editorial

"That Professor Goddard...does not know the relation of action to reaction...would be absurd. Of course, he only seems to lack the knowledge ladled out daily in high schools" - The New York Times, January 1920

  • Everyone Knows
    • Rockets need air to push against.
    • The vacuum of space makes flight impossible.
  • Goddard's challenge
    • Rockets that could operate beyond the atmosphere.
    • In 1920, a New York Times editorial mocked his efforts.

Free Thinking, Scientific Guardrails

Goddard broke free of conventional wisdom, not the laws of physics. Newton's third law and conservation of momentum explain the mechanism: the rocket accelerates one way as expelled exhaust moves the other. It does not need surrounding air to push against. Goddard's vacuum experiments and measurements tested the prediction rather than relying on conviction. Science supplies guardrails that let us check our guesses against reality and reject those that fail.

  • Challenge assumptions
    • Goddard's thinking escaped convention
    • Not the laws of physics.
  • Keep scientific guardrails
    • Momentum is conserved
      • Exhaust goes back \(\rightarrow\) rocket goes forward.
    • Vacuum experiments tested the prediction.
  • Imagine boldly
    • Evidence decides which ideas survive.
